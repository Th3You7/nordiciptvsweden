Around 465,000 Swedish households had access to illegal IPTV in spring 2026 — 175,000 fewer than a year earlier. That is the Swedish headline from Mediavision's latest research into the IPTV Nordic market, reported on 2 September, and it arrives just as streaming prices keep rising and the SHL hockey season opens on 19 September.

If you have moved to Sweden for work or study, those numbers lead to questions most Swedes never have to think about. What are you already paying for TV through your Swedish tax? Does SINK change that? And how do you set up TV here without signing a long contract or ending up with a service you shouldn't trust? This guide answers each one from a Swedish point of view. *Figures and prices checked 13 September 2026.*

## IPTV Nordic from a Swedish address

In Sweden, IPTV — Internet Protocol Television — is less a niche than the default. SVT Play and TV4 Play stream over the internet. So do the TV apps from broadband operators such as Telia and Tele2, and so does a standalone subscription with its own login.

When this guide says IPTV Nordic, it means that shift as it plays out in Sweden: the Swedish numbers, the Swedish prices and the Swedish rules that decide what you pay and how you're protected. Where the wider Nordic research helps explain Sweden we mention it, but the practical advice is written for a household here.

## Mediavision's IPTV Nordic figures for Sweden

According to [Mediavision's figures as reported by MovieZine](https://www.moviezine.se/nyheter/illegal-iptv-minskar-kraftigt-i-sverige-ner-27-procent-pa-ett-ar) (in Swedish), around **465,000 Swedish households** had access to illegal IPTV in spring 2026. A year earlier the figure was **640,000** — a drop of roughly 175,000 households, or about 27 percent, in a single year.

| | Spring 2025 | Spring 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Swedish homes with illegal IPTV | about 640,000 | about 465,000 |
| Change over the year | — | about −175,000 (−27%) |
| Share of all homes | — | just over 1 in 10 |
| Average paid per home | — | SEK 171 a month |
| Money going to illegal services | — | nearly SEK 80m a month, almost SEK 1bn a year |

The fall is real, but the level is still high. Mediavision analyst Adrian Grande put it plainly, as reported by [Dagens PS](https://www.dagensps.se/weekend/noje-underhallning/pirat-tv-tappar-tittare-men-svenskar-betalar-fortfarande-en-miljard-om-aret/) (in Swedish): *"It is positive that the trend is heading in the right direction, but the level is still alarmingly high."*

Why is it falling? Mediavision is careful not to claim it knows. *"We cannot establish why usage is falling, but the increased attention may have affected consumers' attitudes,"* Grande says (our translation). It's an honest answer. Illegal IPTV has been discussed far more in Sweden over the past year, and more households appear to have started asking what they are actually paying for.

## Sweden in the wider Nordic research

Mediavision's IPTV Nordic research also covers Denmark, Finland and Norway. According to [Telecompaper's report on the same research](https://www.telecompaper.com/news/nordic-tv-piracy-access-rate-reaches-1-home-in-10-in-spring-2026--1581635), around **1.2 million homes** across the four countries had access to pirate TV in spring 2026 — nearly one in ten. They pay about €20 a month on average, which comes to **about €280M a year, according to Mediavision**.

For someone living in Sweden, two details matter more than the regional total:

- **Sweden carries a lot of the weight.** At around 465,000 homes, Sweden alone accounts for close to four in ten of the Nordic households in the research.
- **The money isn't small.** SEK 171 a month is roughly what an ordinary streaming subscription costs. People buying an illegal service are usually paying a normal price — without the protection of a real contract, an identifiable seller or a working right to cancel.

That is the heart of the IPTV Nordic question in Sweden this autumn. It's rarely about wanting TV for free. It's about wanting *everything in one place* at a price that feels fair, in a market that makes that surprisingly hard.

## What still reaches a Swedish aerial — and what moved online

If you've arrived from a country where an aerial or a cable box still delivers most channels, Swedish TV can be confusing at first. Almost everything now comes through the internet.

The terrestrial network — TV through an ordinary aerial — is now close to public service only. [According to Bredbandsval](https://www.bredbandsval.se/tv/marksand-tv) (in Swedish), TV4 stopped broadcasting as free-to-air on the terrestrial network in January 2026. What remains through an aerial is SVT1, SVT2, SVT24, SVT Barn and Kunskapskanalen, plus TV Finland in some areas. Boxer ended all pay TV on the terrestrial network on 2 January 2025, and no operator offers pay TV through an aerial in Sweden anymore.

The operators are moving online as well. On 18 August 2026, [The Desk reported](https://thedesk.net/2026/08/viaplay-allente-upgrade-nordic-streaming-markets/) that Viaplay Group had launched a new Allente interface in Sweden and its other Nordic markets. It runs on an AI-enabled Android TV set-top box and brings live TV, on-demand viewing and apps such as HBO Max, SkyShowtime, Prime Video and Apple TV into a single menu.

In practice, **nearly all TV in Sweden is becoming IPTV**, whether it comes from SVT, an operator or a standalone service. That is the IPTV Nordic shift in its most practical form, and for a newcomer it's good news: you don't need an aerial, a satellite dish or a long cable contract. What you need is a stable internet connection and a clear idea of which services you want.

## The public service fee and SINK: what expats in Sweden pay

Sweden abolished its TV licence in 2019 and replaced it with the public service fee, which funds SVT, Sveriges Radio and UR. It isn't tied to owning a TV — it follows your tax status. [According to Skatteverket](https://www.skatteverket.se/privat/skatter/arbeteochinkomst/askattsedelochskattetabeller/publicserviceavgift.4.22501d9e166a8cb399f31dd.html) (in Swedish), the Swedish Tax Agency:

- The fee applies to **everyone over 18 who lives and pays tax in Sweden**, and to people who live abroad but have unlimited tax liability in Sweden and Swedish earned income.
- It's **1 percent of your taxable earned income**, capped at **SEK 1,184 per person for 2026**. The cap was SEK 1,249 in 2025.
- It's **deducted automatically with the rest of your tax**. No bill arrives in the post.
- People with **no taxable earned income** don't pay it.
- People **taxed under SINK** — the special income tax for non-residents — don't pay it either, because they have limited tax liability in Sweden.

### What this means in your first year

Your tax status decides the fee, not your TV — and for expats it's the part of the IPTV Nordic picture that is easiest to get wrong. Three common situations for people who move here:

| Your situation | Public service fee |
|---|---|
| Living in Sweden, working and taxed in the ordinary way | Yes — 1% of taxable earned income, up to SEK 1,184 for 2026 |
| Living abroad, working in Sweden and taxed under SINK | No |
| Living in Sweden with no taxable earned income | No |

Because the fee is a percentage of taxable earned income, someone who starts work partway through the year pays 1 percent of what they actually earned, until the cap is reached. If you're unsure which group you belong to, Skatteverket decides your tax status — check with them rather than relying on a colleague's experience.

Either way, once you're taxed in the ordinary way in Sweden you already fund SVT, and SVT Play is free to use. That's worth knowing before you pay for another service that duplicates it.

## Legal or illegal IPTV in Sweden: five checks before you pay

Whatever you read about the IPTV Nordic market, the technology behind IPTV is completely legal. SVT Play, TV4 Play and the operators' TV services all use it. What matters is whether a service has the right to show what it streams. These are the signs worth checking before you pay for any TV service:

1. **Who runs the service?** A reputable seller shows a company name, a registration number and an address in its terms. If that's missing entirely, treat it as a warning sign.
2. **Can you get your money back?** Read the terms on cancellation and refunds. When you buy at a distance in Sweden, you generally have a 14-day statutory right of withdrawal, [as Konsumentverket explains](https://www.konsumentverket.se/en/process/right-of-withdrawal/).
3. **How do you pay?** Cards and other traceable methods give you protection that cash, cryptocurrency or gift cards don't.
4. **What is being promised?** "Every channel", "all sport" and "works everywhere with no restrictions" are promises nobody can keep, because rights are sold country by country.
5. **Trial and support.** Can you test before paying, and is there someone to ask when something stops working?

## Setting up TV in Sweden without a long contract

Between price rises, a new SHL season and a TV market moving online, there are good reasons to review your setup — or to set one up properly if you've just arrived. Whether you pick an operator, a streaming service or an IPTV subscription, the IPTV Nordic basics are the same:

1. **Add up the whole TV bill.** Include the public service fee if it applies to you, streaming, sport and any operator box. The total is what matters, not a single package price.
2. **Check the contract length.** A 12- or 24-month contract is hard to change when prices or line-ups shift halfway through — and hard to exit if your plans in Sweden change.
3. **Start from what you actually watch.** Don't pay for sports packages during the months your league is on break.
4. **Make sure the service works on your devices** before buying new hardware. The details are on our [supported devices page](/en/devices).
5. **Test before you pay.** A reputable service lets you check quality and stability on your own network — [ask for a free trial](/en/contact) if one isn't offered straight away.
6. **Read the terms.** Who is selling, what happens if you change your mind, and what is actually promised?

Your internet connection is rarely the problem in Sweden. We recommend at least 15 Mbps for HD and 25 Mbps for 4K, and an ordinary fibre or 5G connection comfortably covers both. Stability matters more than peak speed.

IPTV Nordic in Sweden this autumn comes down to four connected points.

**Illegal IPTV is falling.** According to Mediavision, around 465,000 Swedish households had access to illegal IPTV in spring 2026, down from 640,000 a year earlier — a drop of about 27 percent. The level is still high: just over one home in ten, and almost SEK 1 billion a year. Across the Nordics it's around 1.2 million households and about €280M a year, according to Mediavision.

**TV is moving online.** The aerial now carries little beyond SVT, and the operators are switching to internet-based platforms too.

**Your tax status sets your public service fee.** Taxed in the ordinary way in Sweden, you pay up to SEK 1,184 for 2026. Taxed under SINK, you don't.

The practical IPTV Nordic takeaway is to add up your whole TV bill, avoid long contracts — particularly if your time in Sweden is open-ended — and choose a service that is open about what it offers and what it doesn't.
